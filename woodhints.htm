Tricks and Tips

A flat surface to work on is invaluable.  I use the back patio (a concrete slab) or the garage floor.  When I am doing a frame, I cut all four sides of the frame before I put the frame together.  I then lay all four sides out, one end butted up against the patio step or wall of the garage and then I drill the pilot holes and put the screws in the opposite end.  This helps me get the initial frame square / even.  It also gives me something to push against when I am putting the screws into the frame.

Use Deck screws.  Do not use nails.  If you use "normal" nails they will (eventually) pull out and rust.  Also, I used LOTS of Deck Screws when I did these projects.  I used 3, 4 or 5 screws when I put two of the 1.5X7.25 boards together.  Think about whether or not the boards seem to be "structural" or not.  Will they need the strength?  It is easier to put more screws in now than try to add screws after the whole thing is together.

When I built these projects I used Phillips head deck screws because that was the bit I had.  Today: buy star drive (Torx) deck screws instead.  A Phillips head is designed to "cam out" (the bit pops out of the screw under torque) and that is exactly how screw heads get chewed up.  Star drive bits do not cam out, and most deck screws now come with the correct bit in the box.  The chewed–screw–head problem I describe below has been largely engineered away – take advantage of it.  Also learn the clutch setting on your drill (that numbered ring behind the chuck) – set it so the drill stops driving when the screw seats, instead of chewing the head or burying the screw halfway through the board.

When buying deck screws, I generally bought the 5 Lb box of 2.5" screws.  I then bought a 1 or 2 Lb. box of 3" screws and a 1 or 2 Lb box of 1.5" screws, just get an assortment.  Most of the joints I put together were either joining a 1" board to 1.5" board or two 1.5" boards together.  I have the small box of 3" screws and the small box of 2" screws just in case the boards I am joining are thicker or thinner than the 1.5" and 1" boards.  The rule of thumb for screw length: the screw should go about two thirds of its length into the SECOND board – and check the far side, a 3" screw through two 1.5" boards is flush with the far face and will poke through if anything is off.  I use many screws.  Do not be stingy with the number of screws when you are joining boards together.  It is easier to put more screws in while you are building the project than to try to add them when the project is finished and wobbly because you did not use enough screws.

Pilot Holes are a very good thing.  It helps keep the wood from splitting.  It also helps to drive the screws in straight.  The quick rule: the pilot bit should match the screw's solid core (the shank inside the threads) – hold the bit in front of the screw, you should see threads sticking out on both sides but not the core.  Pilot holes matter MOST near the end of a board – that is where wood splits.  A combination pilot–and–countersink bit is a cheap upgrade that does both jobs in one plunge.

Get a tool that allows you to quickly switch from drill bit to driver bit.  At first I would drill the holes, remove the drill bit, put the driver bit onto the drill, screw in some of the decking screws, pull the driver bit and put the drill bit back in (and so on).  A little tedious but it worked.  I found the DeWALT reversible Screw / Drill Bit "Drill Drive" and that helped immensely.  Switching from drill bit to driver bit only takes a few seconds.  I am sure that other manufacturers have created the same (basic) tool.  I do (however) go thru quite a few drill bits and just a few driver bits.  I don't have the drill bit set exactly center in the DeWALT and the drill wobbles a bit until I get it in the wood.

Mark your cuts with a "V" instead of a single tick mark – the point of the V sits exactly on your measurement, where a single line can wander as you draw it.  And remember the saw blade itself eats about 1/8" of wood (the "kerf") – always cut on the WASTE side of your line, and never try to get two finished pieces out of one line.  Measure, cut the first piece, then measure again for the second.  Yes, this is the long way of saying "measure twice, cut once" – everyone says it because everyone has learned it the expensive way.

BE SURE to check the wood and see how it is treated.  If it is older pressure treated wood (pre–2004) it is almost certainly CCA wood – arsenic based, phased out for residential use at the end of 2003 but still in countless existing decks and structures.  See my CCA page for the safety details, and whatever you do, NEVER burn treated wood of any kind.

This is where the pliers come into play.  If you ever chew up the head of a deck screw you have two choices.  If it isn't in too obvious of a spot (or you don't care) you use the Vise-Grip Locking Grip Pliers. Close the end of the pliers until they are a little smaller than the head of the screw.  Clamp down HARD on the head and either tighten the screw or loosen it (if you want to try again with a new screw). You can use the pliers to drive it in flush or you can back the screw out and try with a new screw.

The cheap hotel pens are perfect for marking the length of a board before I cut it.  They never seem to get all gunked up with wood shavings / dirt / etc.  If there is ever any dirt, just drawing a line on a piece of paper just cleans them right up.

All my measurements are in inches.  If you can find a tape measure that has inches on one edge and feet / inches along the other edge that will probably make life easier.

I tried to allow for "slop" in these projects.  Error of 1/8" or 1/4" here and there should not make too much of a difference, but do try to be as accurate as possible.  Very few of the 5.5" by 1" boards I worked with were exactly straight, most had curvature of some sort in them and I still managed to get good fits.  Related: when you are buying lumber, close one eye and sight down the length of every board like a rifle barrel before you put it in the cart.  You will see the bow, crook or twist immediately.  The straight boards are hiding in the middle of the pile, and yes, digging for them is allowed – just restack neatly, the next woodworker thanks you.

I didn't use the clamps at first, but they would have been extremely useful if I had them available.  You can do the work without them but life is easier with them.  The old saying is that no woodworker has ever owned too many clamps, and I have found no evidence against it – a clamp is a third hand that never gets tired and never complains.

If you do not have an old deck out back but you still want to do these projects, check with a local salvage yard for the wood.  Search for "used building materials" or "architectural salvage" (the thing we used to look up in the Yellow Pages), and check whether there is a Habitat for Humanity ReStore near you – used building materials, donated fixtures, and the money goes to a good cause.  Also see if there is a sawmill in the area.  You may be able to get wood that isn't perfect for a very low price.  The porcelain coated cast iron double kitchen sink in the potting bench was $35 at a used Building materials place (The faucet and plastic tubing I attached was much more than that).  Be sure to check the "Used" prices against your local hardware store (like Home Depot or Lowe's).  New might be about the same price as used.

When you need to move these projects around, get 3" PVC pipe and cut into 4 foot sections making 3 of them.  Lift up one end of the project, slide a PVC pipe underneath, roll it forward and slide the next one underneath and finally the last one.  All of these projects can be rolled around the yard using the technique that the Egyptians used to move their large stones around.

All of these projects are downloadable in PowerPoint format and PDF files.  The PowerPoint version is available so that you can modify the projects if you have different kinds of wood than I have used.  Each of the "boards" in the PowerPoint is drawn at a 1 inch = 1 foot scale (unless otherwise noted).  It makes it easier to see if everything is going to fit together before you actually cut the boards.  Some of the ratios I used are in the file Ratios.txt.  To see the "size" of the board in PowerPoint, Right Click on the board you wish to size, then Format Shape (called "Format Autoshape" in the old versions) and look at Size.  The "size" will be the scaled dimension: for example if you want a 12" wide by 24" long board, the width will be 1" and the height will be 2".  If you want a 24.5" high by 15" wide board you would put 2.04" into the "height" box and 1.25" into the "width" box.  Rotation should be 0 (Unless you want the board to be at an angle).
