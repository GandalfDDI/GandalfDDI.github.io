Motorcycle Trip Equipment


This page is about the PHYSICAL stuff – what goes in the panniers, on the seat, and in the dry bag. The decisions (where, when, how far, where you sleep) are over on the Trip Planning page. The two go together: the plan tells you what equipment you need, and the equipment limits what plans are realistic.

Preparing for a trip won't guarantee that the trip will be perfect, but hopefully it will be less ... Adventurous. Every trip you learn a little more to make the next trip better. Take small trips, day trips, then make them longer. You will learn what you need (and more importantly) what you do NOT need. You don't have much for packing, so be careful in what you choose to take... Figure out what you can buy on the road.

There are as many trips as people that go on them. Below I describe packing for just two kinds of trips I take: staying in motels (easier) or motorcycle camping. The only difference between motel rooms and camping is that I ADD equipment when motorcycle camping. None of the below links are affiliate links, all of it is equipment that I bought and I use. I will tell you the good and bad below, or if you ask me.

Remember you can ALWAYS buy something on the road if you leave something behind. Also, I reference directions like they do on a plane or ship – looking forward from behind: Port (4 letters) is left, Starboard is right.

Also try to think about using the same thing in two different ways. My dry bag not only stores my clothes but also acts as a pillow, with the hoodie I carry adding a little extra padding. My gear sits at the bottom of the tent and acts as a leg rest if I need it or extra covers if it gets too cold. The tent is a backrest for me while I ride.

GPS / Navigation Hardware

Worst case you COULD travel with only a phone navigation system. But you are hoping that your phone will always have a signal – not guaranteed out in the middle of nowhere. My first navigation is a Garmin Zumo GPS for the motorcycle, VERY glad I got one. I bought one used 'just in case' but in the end it worked very nicely. This GPS has the LM (Lifetime Maps) designation – look for that and you won't have to worry about buying maps every few years. Two issues. First, as far as I know you cannot make a circular route (start and end at the same point) – you have to create two separate routes. Second, with mine, the glare. This one is mounted on the Harley.






My second Garmin is a Zumo XT2, Lifetime Maps and a much better screen. You can see the ball mount behind it that I bought to mount it on the Triumph. I mounted the GPS on the handlebars using a Handlebar Ball Mount. While I could probably clean up the wires a little better it works for now.

How to BUILD routes and get them onto the XT2 (including the Basecamp workaround) is on the Trip Planning page, Step 9.



Travel Light – Stay in Motels

The fastest and easiest way to travel is to stay in hotels / motels with a minimal amount of baggage. I would suggest, at a minimum, the GPS and the repair and maintenance kit. In fact I carry the maintenance kit with me on a regular basis.

Repair and Maintenance – Port Side (left), looking forward, Saddle Bag / Pannier

These are the items I put in my left side pannier, the pannier I only open rarely.
  • CyclePump Tire Inflator. Having this and a tire repair kit saved me on a 3500 mile trip when my tire deflated in the middle of nowhere. Fixed the tire and was on my way.
  • Inside the CyclePump bag I also have:
    • Small hammer with a claw to pull out nails and hammer in the plug
    • A Digital Tire Pressure gauge, a cheap one works well. If you lose it any Auto Zone or WalMart should have one.
    • The tire repair kit I have is a simple nail coated in rubber that you pound into the hole. It is for tubeless tires (I have a tube now, we will see if it works or not). I could not find that kit any more but you DO want something that will fix your tire quickly. The Mushroom Plug kit and Rubber Plug kit seem to be popular. The caveat is that on a motorcycle you MUST get your tire repaired as soon as you can. If you get a puncture in your tire, don't just plug/patch the tire and assume you are good. Manufacturers have different guidance on what you should do, some say replace immediately. Worst case, since you are on a motorcycle, you should consider replacing as soon as possible. When a tire is punctured it is no longer rated at the speed rating listed on the tire.
  • A bag of tools that fit the bike I am riding. Many times the bike will come with a manufacturer bag. Or you can buy a kit from the manufacturer (like Harley). You will figure out what you need for your repair kit but DEFINITELY have Cable Ties, duct tape (I like Gorilla Tape). There are LOTS of opinions on what a good Emergency Tool Kit looks like.
  • A Motorcycle Side Stand Puck. The puck is the yellow 10 sided round thing in the picture. You put this under your kickstand when you are on soft sand or dirt so that your jiffy / kick / side stand doesn't sink into the sand and your bike falls over.
  • A Battery Tender / Charger. This allows you to charge your battery in case you mess up & leave something on during the night. Hopefully there is power nearby. Again, I only used it once but once was enough.
  • Speaking of charging a battery, depending on how much you trust your battery, a portable jump starter will keep you from being stranded out in the middle of nowhere.
  • A Rain cover. Keeps the bike dry at night or if you get caught in a storm with little shelter.
  • A fuel bladder IF the route has long empty stretches – whether you need one is a route decision, see the Trip Planning page, Step 7.

Luggage – Starboard Side, looking forward, Pannier / Saddle Bag

I usually carry the items I need quick access to in the right pannier. A long-sleeve hoodie and a rain jacket in case it gets cold or starts raining.

I also keep drinks / water in the saddle bag. When camping (see below) I keep the other loose items (JetBoil, bear spray, propane canister) in the pannier also.

Luggage – Top

It is VERY important when strapping bags or the tent to your motorcycle that you do NOT use bungee cords. They STREEEETCH when you go around corners and your load may start moving around. I use 1 inch straps with a buckle and tighten them so that they do not come loose.

Vendors make bags that fit your panniers perfectly so that you can pack everything in the bag then slide it into the panniers. I had these bags for the K1600 and they worked perfectly, but they were inside the top box and side panniers, protected from weather. The problem was that when I strapped that bag on the Harley pillion seat, since the bag was not waterproof, everything inside got wet. I bought a 65 Liter Big River Dry Bag at REI and that has worked perfectly for me. Note that I have 4 ea. 1 inch straps to hold just that bag on.









Inside the dry bag I put:
  • A bag that holds tooth paste, tooth brush, medicine, shampoo, nail clipper, shaver, everything for the morning routine
  • Shirt, socks, underwear for each day – Usually 4 shirts (you are wearing the fifth one), max 7 days of underwear
  • Pants – I can usually wear a pair of pants for 2 days in a row or so, one pair of pants (you are wearing the other pair)
  • Pillowcase – To put dirty clothes into
  • A pair of Minnetonka Moccasins so that if you have to get up late at night, you don't have to pull on your motorcycle boots
  • Towel – If you will be camping
  • Extra Large (adult) wipes – If you will be camping so that you can clean up somewhat if no shower is available
  • If the trip is more than 4 days, I will have to do laundry – And in that case small HE (high efficiency) sheets for the Laundromat, bottles of any softener or other wash products (IN THEIR OWN SEALED BAGS FOR WHEN THEY LEAK), and LOTS of quarters, usually around $3-$6/load (wash and dry)

Luggage – Misc

I bring along my computer so that I can transfer each day's video over to a hard drive. I put the computer bag in either the dry bag or I put it in the right pannier if there is room and I am out camping.

I always run my camera on my shoulder, hence the need for the laptop.

In that large computer bag is:
  • A very small laptop – This is why I like the Microsoft Surface computer, very small and leaves lots of room in the laptop case. You can get one cheap on EBay in case you are worried about breaking it.
  • Portable hard drive to back up all your pictures and videos
  • A couple rechargeable power banks for the camera, Bluetooth helmet headset and phone. You especially will need at least two if you are going camping. I bring along 2 ea. 30000 or 40000 mAh power banks. Don't look for the absolute cheapest and lightest. They may have a power capacity MUCH LOWER than what they are telling you it is.
  • A good USB charger for when you DO have power available to charge everything.
  • Extra USB cords
  • Extension cord

Also a CamelBak backpack to hold 2L of water so that if anything happens I have water or if I am camping I have a water supply. In the west there are LOTS of long desolate lonely highways. Choose a color other than black, black absorbs the sun (I messed up when I chose mine). The other option (pictured below) is the Source Razor 3L Low Profile Hydration Pack. I like that one better, less dangling straps than the Camelback BUT not much for doubling as a small backpack like the CamelBak does.

Hiding smells on your bike – odor-barrier bags

A bear's nose is the best sensor in the forest – they can smell food from miles away, and "food" to a bear includes your toothpaste, sunscreen, and that honey-peanut-butter container. Abscent bags (activated-carbon lined, the same technology as OPSAK) seal the smell in, and I use them for food and anything scented that rides in the panniers or sits in camp. Two things to understand clearly: FIRST, an odor bag is a layer, not a vault – it lowers your scent signature so you attract less attention, but no bag survives a bear that already knows food is there. SECOND, in national parks the food storage RULES still apply – where the park requires a bear canister or a food locker (Yosemite and Sequoia / Kings Canyon among others), a smell-proof bag does NOT satisfy the regulation, because rangers cannot certify what a bag does and bears have learned what coolers and bags LOOK like. So the play is both: odor bag INSIDE the canister or locker. The bag keeps the bear from coming to look; the hard container is what wins if it looks anyway. A bear's nose beats a bloodhound's by roughly 7x, and can pick up a food smell from up to 20 miles downwind – which is why an odor bag reduces your scent but a canister is what actually stops the bear.

When you get to the campsite

Setting up camp correctly is important. Ron Lieback has some hard-learned lessons about camping out in nature, summarized below but the article has TONS more detail, take some time and read his article:
  • Don't drink heavily. Not sleeping well and dehydration are a bad mix for motorcycles
  • Good camping shoes and a light, even if it is the phone 'flash' light
  • Keep Motorcycle Luggage Away from the Tent
  • Always Have a Medical Kit – Good for when you are out in the middle of nowhere at night and when you are riding in the middle of nowhere during the day
  • Keep a pee jug in your tent, easy to get to & use rather than having to walk around & trip over something in the darkness
  • Park the bike away from where you are sleeping so that it doesn't fall on you. Get a Motorcycle Kickstand Pad / Puck / Plate to stabilize the bike on soft ground.
  • Triple-Check the Ground for bugs, rocks or wet spots before you decide where to pitch your tent
  • If you are hurt, better to call someone than to never be heard from again. If you are going to be out in the middle of nowhere think about something like Garmin InReach. The check-in PROTOCOL that goes with the device is on the Trip Planning page, Step 13.
  • Stay Hydrated and eat. Your brain needs fuel to work properly. Ron brings electrolyte packets and easy snacks (think nuts and dried fruit). I have a container of honey and peanut butter mixed together 50 percent each, no need to refrigerate
  • Pack for all kinds of weather. Insulated sleeping bags, thermal liners, rainproof. Late July on the Continental Divide I ran into COLD RAINY weather
  • Set up your tent first. The middle of nowhere at midnight is not the time to learn you forgot a critical piece of equipment at home
  • Earplugs or white noise can help you get much-needed sleep for the next day
  • Backup Battery or Power Bank – For when you need to keep your phone charged for emergencies or for mapping. You should consider carrying a paper map in case your electronic devices fail
  • Dry Bag for Valuables – Keeps your electronics, wallet, etc dry in case of weather

Panoramic Pictures

My panorama pictures are spliced together using Image Composite Editor, take at least 9 pictures and up to 20 pictures of the landscape and ICE splices them together:

The GREAT things I like about these pictures are:
  • All of the picture is in focus including the grass at the bottom of pictures
  • If you take the picture correctly, the clouds are the right color (not too bright) and the trees on the mountain are visible and the correct color of green
  • There is more detail in the picture as you zoom in
  • I also use Gimp to 'edit' out other people that are in the pictures using the 'clone' tool. If you see a repeated pattern in my picture, that is probably why
  • There are issues
    • The pictures do not always align well (I use Gimp to fix those issues)
    • They DO come out HUUUGE (but Gimp can fix that also)
    • Use the ICE Automatic image completion to fill in parts of the picture that need to be filled in
    • The shadows sometimes do not align / have the same shades


Untried But Interesting – YMMV

Everything above this line I own and use. Everything BELOW this line I have NOT tried – these are things that look interesting, that other riders vouch for, or that I am watching. Your Mileage May Vary, and if you have direct experience with any of them, send me e-mail (address at the bottom) and tell me the good and the bad:
  • CarPlay / Android Auto displays – Units like the Chigee AIO-5 put your PHONE's navigation on a proper sunlight-readable bike display, and the interesting part is that many bundle TPMS (tire pressure monitoring) and front / rear dash cameras into the same unit. The trade against a dedicated GPS like my Zumo: you are back to depending on the phone having signal and battery. Riders seem to love them; I like my routes living ON the device.
  • TPMS by itself – Valve-cap tire pressure sensors that talk to a small display or your phone. Given that my CyclePump story above STARTED with a slow leak in the middle of nowhere, early warning on pressure is not a gadget, it is instrumentation.
  • Minimalist navigation – The Beeline Moto II is a tiny arrow-and-distance puck instead of a full map. Wrong tool for a 9-day EZ66-style route, possibly the RIGHT tool for a wander where you only need a nudge toward tonight's town.
  • Phone mounts and your camera – a warning even though I haven't tried it: bar-mounting a phone exposes its camera to engine vibration that can DESTROY the optical image stabilization – the camera never focuses right again. This is common enough that Quad Lock sells a vibration dampener specifically for it. If you navigate by phone on the bars, use a dampened mount. Full stop.
  • Motorcycle 'garage' tents – The Redverz Atacama and Lone Rider MotoTent have a vestibule the BIKE parks inside – bike out of the weather, gear stays on the bike, standing headroom. Heavy and bulky like my Toucan, so same trade: great for basecamp-style stays, a pain for a new camp every night.
  • Heated gear – Heated grips I have; full heated jacket liners and glove liners running off the bike's electrical system are the next step for shoulder-season riding. The thing to check is your bike's spare alternator capacity before stacking heated everything on it.
  • Phone satellite SOS – Recent phones can send emergency messages by satellite with no cell signal. It is NOT an InReach replacement – no routine check-in messaging on most implementations, and it depends on the phone surviving the crash – but it changes the math on what the minimum safety floor is. Know what YOUR phone can do before deciding you need more.
