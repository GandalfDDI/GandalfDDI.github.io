Trip Day Twenty – Tuesday, September 1, 2026: Great Sand Dunes National Park (bike stayed parked)

Great Sand Dunes NP from the grasslands – from a distance you can see how the dune field is tucked into a pocket at the foot of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains (right), where the opposing winds trap it, while the flat San Luis Valley it came from stretches away to the left. In the foreground, rabbitbrush in its late-summer yellow bloom.


Sand eroded from the San Juan and Sangre de Cristo Mountains, settled in ancient lakes on the valley floor, and has been piled against the Sangre de Cristos by opposing winds ever since. The tallest, Star Dune, is about 750 feet. This is the dry bed of Medano Creek, which flows across here in late spring with the Great Sand Dunes behind:

At the city limits of Los Alamos I stopped and got a picture of one of the original Los Alamos guard gates that they have kept preserved:


I had to go to Los Alamos, not only for the atomic bomb history but for the National Laboratory itself. What I did not realize is that NM 501 runs right through the Lab – suddenly I was at a gate with armed guards and thought I had taken a wrong turn. Nope: it is one of the Lab's vehicle checkpoints on a public road. Turn off the camera, show your driver's license, and on you go. (Vehicles can also be pulled aside for random inspection; refuse, and they send you the long way around on NM 4.) They had a building & statues of the men who organized the atomic bombs:


Frijoles Canyon, Bandelier National Monument, from the Entrance Road on the north rim – across the canyon, the south wall shows the same soft volcanic tuff. The Ancestral Pueblo people carved their rooms into the sun-facing north wall, just upstream from here, and built Tyuonyi pueblo on the canyon floor:


Bandelier National Monument, Frijoles Canyon – these aren't caves. The cliff is soft volcanic tuff from the Valles Caldera eruptions, and between roughly 1150 and the mid-1500s Ancestral Pueblo people carved these rooms (cavates) into it. Most were the back rooms of multi-story stone houses built against the cliff; the masonry has long since fallen, but the rows of beam holes above the openings show where the floors and roofs once were. Their descendants live today at Cochiti, San Ildefonso and the other Rio Grande pueblos:


Valle Grande, Valles Caldera National Preserve – the grassy floor of a 13-mile-wide volcanic caldera, ringed by forested peaks. Hollywood noticed: The Missing (2003) and Disney's The Lone Ranger (2013) both filmed here, and the log cabin from The Missing still stands just up the entrance road:
