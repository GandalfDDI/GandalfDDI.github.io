Trip Day Thirty-five – Wednesday, September 16, 2026: Yuma, Arizona to Joshua Tree National Park (262 miles)

The Salton Sea sits in the Salton Trough, a rift valley where the San Andreas Fault meets the spreading zone that opens down into the Gulf of California. The floor is more than 230 feet below sea level. Over thousands of years the Colorado River built a delta and moved away from the Salton Sea sealing it off from the Gulf. The last version was gone by around 1700. The modern sea is an accident. In 1905, spring floods blew out a poorly built irrigation system that had been cut into the Colorado's bank to water the Imperial Valley, and for nearly two years the entire river poured into the dry Salton Sink, drowning a salt mine and a stretch of railroad before the Southern Pacific Railroad closed the breach in 1907. With no outlet, the sea has survived since then mostly on agricultural runoff but since the 2000s that water has been sold to cities not farms. Evaporation has left it roughly twice as salty as the ocean. Since the 2000s the Salton Sea has been shrinking, exposing dusty lakebed, killing fish, and emptying resort towns like Bombay Beach that boomed there in the 1950s:

Joshua Tree National Park, these trees are so unique. They aren't trees at all – they're a giant yucca related to agaves. They grow wild only in the Mojave Desert between about 2,000 and 6,000 feet. The park sits where the higher Mojave meets the lower, hotter Colorado Desert. The Joshua trees fill the western half and almost disappear as you drive east and downhill. Mormon pioneers in the 1850s supposedly named them after the prophet Joshua – the branches looking like arms raised in prayer. They grow only a few inches a year and have no growth rings, so their age has to be estimated, the big ones are thought to be several hundred years old:

Trip Day Thirty-seven – Friday, September 18, 2026: Death Valley National Park (49 miles)

I rode around Death Valley (early in the morning) to Zabriskie Point and Badwater Basin. Zabriskie Point is named for Christian Brevoort Zabriskie, vice president and general manager of the Pacific Coast Borax Company, whose 20-mule teams hauled borax (I always wondered about the 20 mule teams on the washing boxes ...) out of Death Valley in the 1880s. The company's road crews named the viewpoint after him in the 1920s. The golden, wrinkled hills are mud and ash laid down in an ancient lake 5 to 9 million years ago, later tilted and eroded, with Manly Beacon as the tall peak just right of center.

Throughout the western states you hear about 'Bad Lands'. I saw them in Petrified Forest and Death Valley, but I first ran into them in South Dakota at the Badlands National Park. Looking up the designation, 'Badlands' goes back to the Lakota name for South Dakota's badlands, makȟóšiča, meaning 'bad land,' and to French trappers who called them les mauvaises terres à traverser, 'bad lands to cross.' Today it's a geologic term for soft, clay-rich sedimentary rock in a dry climate with almost no plants to hold it together. The rare cloudbursts cut it into gullies, ridges and rills, so badlands turn up all over the arid West: Death Valley, South Dakota, Theodore Roosevelt in North Dakota, Petrified Forest and Capitol Reef among them:
