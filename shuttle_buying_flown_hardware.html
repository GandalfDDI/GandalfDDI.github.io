Buying "Flown" Space Shuttle Hardware On The Internet

A field guide to what the collector market calls "proof", written by someone who spent ~11 years at Kennedy Space Center working Shuttle launch operations (MPS/SSME hardware, Firing Room console). I went browsing "Space Shuttle Flown Hardware" listings on the usual online marketplaces (eBay, Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, Mercari, the auction aggregator sites – pick your poison, the pathology is identical on all of them) out of semi somewhat morbid curiosity about what people are trying to pass off as flown Space Shuttle Hardware. What I found was worth writing down. Short version: almost none of it is what the listing says, most sellers genuinely don't know what they have, and the price has almost nothing to do with whether the item ever left the ground.


The Four Questions

Every listing collapses five separate questions into one word: "FLOWN". They are not one question. They are four, and each one requires different evidence:
  • Is it real aerospace hardware? Usually yes. Part numbers, materials, and workmanship are hard to fake and mostly nobody bothers. This is the question the seller CAN answer, and it is the least valuable one.
  • Is it Shuttle? Check the part number prefix (see below). This one is free to answer and most sellers get it wrong anyway.
  • Was it installed on an Orbiter? Requires paperwork, or a QA tag written against an installed configuration. Installed does NOT mean flown – quite a number of parts on the orbiter got installed, inspected, dinged, pulled and replaced during a processing flow without ever leaving the ground.
  • Did it fly? Is my Space Shuttle part really flown? – Requires a document naming the vehicle and the mission. Nothing else answers this question. Not wear, not discoloration, not soot, not the seller's confidence, not a sticker.
  • What does the part number tell me What does V070 mean on a NASA part? – See below for what a part number tells you and what KIND of vehicle (or not) it MIGHT have been on
When you see "FLOWN" on a listing, ask yourself which of the four questions the evidence shown actually answers. It is almost always only the first one.

Part Numbers - The Free Authentication Nobody Uses

The companies involved in manufacture are diverse. Rockwell International's Space Division in Downey built the Orbiter (though check the date, because that same outfit was North American Aviation until 1967 and North American Rockwell until 1973, and Boeing after 1996), with Rocketdyne doing the SSMEs, Thiokol in Utah casting the SRB motors (branded Morton Thiokol from 1982 to 1989, which is the window that includes Challenger), USBI assembling and recovering the boosters, Martin Marietta at Michoud fabricating the External Tank (and only becoming Lockheed Martin after the 1995 merger, so an ET part with "Lockheed Martin" on it is late or wrong), Lockheed Missiles & Space making the tiles, Aerojet the OMS engines, Marquardt the RCS thrusters, Sundstrand the APUs, Pratt & Whitney the fuel cells, and Spar Aerospace in Canada the RMS arm; go back a program and it is North American Aviation in Downey on the Apollo Command and Service Module, Grumman at Bethpage on the Lunar Module, Boeing at Michoud on the Saturn V S-IC first stage, North American at Seal Beach on the S-II second stage (that is your V7- prefix right there, see below), Douglas at Huntington Beach on the S-IVB, Chrysler on the Saturn IB first stage, IBM in Huntsville on the Instrument Unit, Rocketdyne again on the F-1 and J-2 engines, Lockheed Propulsion on the escape tower, ILC Dover on the A7L suit and Hamilton Standard on its backpack, and McDonnell Douglas on the Skylab workshop; go back further still and it is McDonnell in St. Louis on both Mercury and Gemini, Chrysler on Redstone, Convair on Atlas, Martin on Titan II, and Lockheed on Agena – which is a long way of saying that the manufacturer name and the program name have to overlap in time, and when a listing pairs "North American Aviation" with "Space Shuttle Orbiter" it has convicted itself in a single line without any help from you. Likewise saying 'Apollo Space Shuttle Bolt' makes absolutely no sense.

Drawing numbers tell you the program and whether it is vehicle or ground hardware, and they are stamped right on the part. I did not do avionics, but in ~11 years I do not remember seeing flight hardware with anything but these formats:
  • V070-XXXXXX – Space Shuttle Orbiter, structures. V072, V074, etc. are other Orbiter systems. The letter, a THREE DIGIT system block, a six digit base number, then a dash number. The three digit block is not optional.
  • V7-XXXXXX or V36-XXXXXX – North American Aviation, Apollo era. V7 is the Saturn S-II neighborhood, V36 is CSM. Same visual DNA as Shuttle numbers because it is the direct ancestor of the same drawing system. One missing zero is the difference between programs. Sellers see "V7-317747" and type "Space Shuttle" because the number LOOKS right. It is an Apollo era number.
  • G070-XXXXXX – Ground Support Equipment. G for Ground, V for Vehicle. I watched a G070 lifting bracket, stenciled "PROOF LOADED / CAP 500 LBS" (you proof load slings and handling fixtures, you do not proof load flight structure), sell as "Flown NASA Space Shuttle Bracket Assembly". The object announced what it was in two independent ways and the listing contradicted both.
  • KSC C XXXXX – That is not a part number at all. That is a KSC calibration inventory control number. I watched a Proto torque wrench wearing an out-of-calibration status band sell for $850 as a "NASA Space Shuttle Proto Torque Wrench", with its cal sticker number quoted as the part number. It was a hand tool that failed calibration. It brought the highest price in the entire auction.
  • No number at all – Standard NAS/MS hardware. A drilled-head bolt with a torque stripe is on Cessnas, forklifts, hydraulic test stands, and every turbine housing in America. The drilled head is for lockwire, the paint stripe is a witness mark so an inspector can see the fastener has not backed off. Both are ubiquitous across all of aviation. An unserialized standard fastener can NEVER be tied to a specific vehicle. Not "hard to authenticate" – impossible, by design. Standard parts are the same object in a hundred thousand applications. Anyone selling a loose bolt in a baggie labeled "FLOWN SHUTTLE ORBITER BOLT" is selling you a Sharpie autograph on a sandwich bag.
Also: External Tank hardware CANNOT be flown-and-recovered. The ET was expended on every single flight and broke up over the ocean. Anything on Earth that is genuinely ET hardware is by definition spare, test, or production stock. A listing that says "space-flown external tank" is wrong about one of those two words, guaranteed.

The Tags - Learn Two Colors

This is the part nobody outside the program writes down, and every listing I looked at got it backwards.
  • RED tag, KSC Form 2-155A, Disposal Document – This is the GOOD one. Scrap disposition is the formal act by which the government extinguishes its ownership of an object. A red SCRAP tag with the 2-155A paperwork means title legally passed and the item is clean to buy and sell. It is also frequently the ONLY document tying a part to a vehicle and mission. I have seen a tile fragment with its 2-155A naming OV-103 and the STS-85 removal – that scrap paperwork is worth more than the tile.
  • ORANGE tag, KSC Form 23-244, Non-Conforming Material – This is a QA hold tag. It means a technician or inspector found something wrong – the part was flagged for defect, with a Defect Report number, pending disposition. It says right on the form "To be removed only by originating quality inspection organization". It is NOT a scrap tag. Sellers routinely describe the orange tag as a "red scrap tag" because scrap is the word that makes the sale legal-sounding. The colors are not interchangeable and neither are the meanings.
Here is the interesting wrinkle: an orange 23-244 wired to a part is actually decent evidence the part was INSTALLED, because defect reports get written against installed configurations – line gets dinged during processing, QA inspects and tags it, part gets removed and replaced. Orbiter hardware? Yes. Flown? Who knows – all you know is that it likely came off during a ground flow, it may have been installed and came right off the vehicle it was on before the part launched again. Installed and flown are different questions. The collector market has no vocabulary for "genuine Orbiter part, installed, removed under paperwork, zero flight time" – which describes a very large population of parts. Same with a part marked 'Serviceable Tag' R.T.S. (Return To Service). It means the part was checked out of inventory and returned to inventory.

And remember that used-looking is not flown-looking. Pyro devices are lot-acceptance tested – samples out of every production lot get fired on a test stand. A fired separation component from a testbed is physically identical to one that fired in flight, especially the ones that live in sealed cavities behind doors that close before entry. Some hardware categories have NO flight signature at all. Soot proves a firing, not a flight.

What The Money Actually Says

In 2000, Charles Bell's estate was auctioned off on Merritt Island. Bell was a NASA design engineer at KSC for 37 years who spent decades buying NASA surplus – fifty-six tractor-trailer containers of it across 7 acres. I went to some of those Ransom Road surplus auctions myself (for cars... interesting... alot of trash, some good stuff but not much). One buyer from Charles Bell's collection took several trailers home, sat on them for twenty years, then consigned ~900 lots through Puckett Auctions of Muncie, Indiana across four sales in summer 2020. The realized prices are public, and they are the only measured dataset that exists for this material. Read that table twice. The correctly numbered, genuinely Orbiter parts are all at the BOTTOM. The top of the market was a broken hand tool and a strip of webbing. The entire Orb/ET separation interface – bolt, compression disk, canister, frangible nut, umbilical gasket, all almost certainly used flight-class hardware – sold for $1,125 combined. The torque wrench alone brought $850.

The rule this teaches: buyers pay for what they can IMAGINE, not for what flew. Everyone can picture a tech at the Cape holding a torque wrench. Nobody can picture themselves holding a monoball gasket, because you have to already know what one is. Price tracks recognizability, and recognizability is inversely correlated with flight likelihood, because real flight hardware is unmarked, unglamorous, and requires somebody who worked the aft compartment to tell you what it is.

Six years later, that same $275 canister was back online for $1,700 with a new description ("external fuel tank liner" – wrong, it is Orbiter-side, the ET was never recovered) and the same missing paperwork. The QA card that sold for $60 with two clips on it was relisted with the clips REMOVED and a laminated sticker added reading "FLOWN IN SPACE ONBOARD SHUTTLE FOR MULTIPLE MISSION". The only genuine artifacts on the card were subtracted, and the price went up. That is the market you are shopping in.

Is a Certificate of Authenticity worth anything?

A Certificate Of Authenticity is worth exactly as much as (a) what the signer was in a position to know, and (b) what the certificate specifically claims. Run every COA through those two filters:
  • "Verified as flown by Ken Havekotte though not mission specific or time of removal" – an actual quote from a listing. Read it again. No vehicle, no mission, no removal date. What is left that could have been verified? That sentence eats itself. Havekotte is a genuinely respected name in the space collecting world, and that is exactly why his name is on the listing – but the same listing admits he bought the item "via space auction, or like auction decades ago", meaning even HE does not know which sale it came from. The expert authenticated the TYPE of hardware and the ERA. Type and era are not flight history. A real name attached to an unanswerable question is still an unanswered question.
  • "Spoke directly with the framer and he guaranteed his work and his relationship working with NASA!" – an actual quote from a $1,500 listing. The framer can attest that he framed it. NASA did not fly the frame.
  • "Collage is 100% authentic" – the broadest possible word attached to the narrowest possible object. The ASSEMBLY is authentic. Nobody said the flags flew.
  • "Ex: Charles Bell Collection" – this is genuine provenance for "came from an uncatalogued pile on Merritt Island" and zero provenance for "flew". Bell was a KSC design engineer for 37 years who bought NASA surplus by the trailer for decades because he could not stand watching the agency landfill its own history – and good on him for it. But the material was formally severed from its records BEFORE he bought it; that is what surplus disposition IS. Most of what he saved was spares, shop stock, GSE, test articles, QA retention samples, and at least one Navy Polaris component. So here is the rule: even if the item genuinely DID pass through Charles Bell's trailers or Ken Havekotte's inventory – and even if the listing SAYS it flew – the pedigree tells you who OWNED it, not what it DID. Bell never had the paperwork. Havekotte, by his own listing's admission, sometimes does not know which auction a piece came from. A famous previous owner is a chain of custody for the object, not a flight log for it. Provenance of possession and provenance of flight are two different documents, and the market only ever shows you the first one.
  • A modern checkbox card ("Era: Mercury / Gemini / Apollo / Shuttle / Titan / Atlas – Category: Flight-Flown / GSE") is somebody's private inventory worksheet, written decades after the fact. Notice the form has no "Unknown" box. Given a pyro nut and two choices, you check Flight/Flown because it sure isn't GSE. The form MANUFACTURES certainty because uncertainty is not one of the options. NASA never printed a form that offers you a menu of six programs.
The only COA that means anything is a period NASA/contractor document – a 2-155A, a removal PR, a logbook page – naming a vehicle, and ideally a mission, that matches the part in your hand. If the seller has that, the paperwork is what you are buying and the metal came free. If the seller does not have that, you are buying metal.

And here is the part most people do not know: you cannot write to NASA and ask. We kept meticulous paperwork on every part while it was in the system – and then the records met their retention schedules and were lawfully destroyed, same as the hardware was lawfully surplused. Two disposal processes, both working exactly as designed, neither one aware of the other. The processing paperwork that survives in a collector's frame is not a receipt. It is the ONLY surviving copy of the record. The paperwork IS the archive.

Exporting This Stuff - The Legal Hazard Nobody In The Market Knows Exists

The obligatory disclaimer: I am not a lawyer, this is not legal advice, and anyone actually shipping hardware internationally should get a real classification (a CCATS request through BIS costs nothing but time) rather than trusting a web page. That said, here is the landscape, because I watched a seller offer genuine Orbiter separation-interface hardware with "free shipping to any Domestic or International location" and I am fairly sure they have no idea what they are holding:
  • Retirement did not decontrol the hardware. Spacecraft parts "specially designed" for a spacecraft are export controlled under the EAR (the 2014 reform moved most of them from the ITAR munitions list to Commerce ECCN 9A515 – different administration, still controlled). There is no sunset because the program ended, and no exemption because the part is forty years old. The public-domain carve-outs are for technical DATA, not objects.
  • Not everything is controlled equally. A specially designed Orbiter component (pyro separation hardware, structural interface parts) is the real exposure. Standard NAS/MS fasteners release out of "specially designed" and are almost certainly EAR99 – ship the bolt anywhere non-embargoed. Beta cloth covers, RBF streamers, handling GSE: not spacecraft components, non-issue. The irony is that the hardware most likely to be genuinely flight-class is exactly the hardware most likely to be controlled.
  • YOU are the exporter of record. Not your freight forwarder, not the shipping company, not the auction platform. That boilerplate in every listing about "consignee is responsible for all Duties, Taxes, Customs Clearance" is about the IMPORT side – the buyer's country's fees. It says nothing about whether the item can lawfully LEAVE the United States, and it transfers zero liability off the seller.
  • China is a statutory wall, not a paperwork problem. By act of Congress (FY13 NDAA), spacecraft items subject to the EAR may not be exported or transferred, directly or INDIRECTLY, to China, North Korea, or state sponsors of terrorism, absent a presidential waiver. There is no license to apply for. Russia post-2022 is functionally the same under a policy of denial, and is a top enforcement priority besides. And note the "indirectly" – selling to a domestic buyer you have reason to believe is forwarding it is the same offense, and even transferring OWNERSHIP to a foreign national from those countries can itself constitute the controlled export before the box moves an inch.
  • Liability is strict and does not expire at your border. Civil penalties do not require intent – "I did not know" is not a defense – and run to hundreds of thousands of dollars per violation or twice the transaction value. Willful violations are criminal: up to $1M and 20 years, plus the companion charges that ride along (smuggling, false export filings). US-origin hardware stays subject to the EAR forever, wherever it goes – the collector in another country who resells it onward to a prohibited destination becomes the violator themselves, years later, having signed nothing.
  • Will anyone actually notice one collectible? Realistically, enforcement finds PEOPLE, not packages – cases come from buyers who are already on watch lists, not from a $300 gasket in the mail. But the one tool the government uses freely costs it nothing: the denied-parties lists, which are public, permanent, and end your ability to touch US-origin goods forever. For a seller, the arithmetic is simple: "US buyers only" costs you a few foreign bids. Getting it wrong costs more than everything in your collection.
  • And the part that should offend an engineer: a spent 1980s pyro sleeve offers zero technical uplift to anybody in 2026 – every spacefaring nation has had mature separation systems for decades. The control captures the object by what it was DESIGNED for, not by any assessment of what an adversary could learn from it. The regime protects nothing at this scale, and it will still ruin your year. Both things are true. Plan accordingly.

The Buying Rules, Condensed

  • Is it a framed picture / flag that you like? After multiple flights NASA sent commemorative "flown flags" to their employees. LOTS of them. Hundreds of flags if not thousands. They usually sell for around $300. Most likely authentic, is it worth $300 to you?
  • Autographed pictures is a completely different game, you will need to dig into that yourself.
  • Buy the documentation, not the metal. A tile chip with its 2-155A naming the vehicle and mission is worth more than a $1,700 mystery casting, and the market prices it exactly backwards. That is an inefficiency. Exploit it.
  • Read the part number before you read the description. V070/V072/V074 = Orbiter. V7/V36 = Apollo era. G070 = ground equipment. KSC C = a cal sticker. No number = unknowable forever.
  • Red 2-155A = legally released, and often the only vehicle/mission link in existence. Orange 23-244 = QA defect hold, probably evidence of installation, probably NOT evidence of flight, and definitely not a scrap tag no matter what the listing says.
  • Fired is not flown. Worn is not flown. Installed is not flown. Sooty is not flown. Only paper is flown.
  • ET (External Tank, the big orange thing in the middle) hardware on Earth did not fly. Full stop. The ET detached and burned up after each flight.
  • SRB (Solid Rocket Boosters, the white boosters on the side of the vehicle) hardware flew up to 150,000 ft (28 mi; 46 km). The definition of 'space' is either 100 km / 62 miles – the Kármán line or 80 km / 50 miles – the US definition. And the hold down posts / hold down bolts don't fly with the boosters. They stay on earth.
  • Ask prices carry no information, on any platform. A fixed-price listing costs nothing to leave up for years – check the sold/completed listings, not the asks (on eBay also check the listing revision history, one click from any listing, nobody ever opens it). The 2020 wholesale numbers above are what this material actually clears at when a room full of people bid on it.
  • If a listing invokes a name – Bell, a famous collector, a framer, an astronaut – ask what that person was actually in a position to KNOW about this specific object. The answer is usually "that it existed".
  • If you are the seller: anything genuinely Orbiter-specific is potentially export controlled – see the export section above. "US bidders only" is not paranoia, it is the only sane default for hardware you cannot classify.

None of this means don't buy. I enjoy this stuff, obviously – I spent an evening identifying the entire Orb/ET separation interface scattered across auction listings by sellers who called the pieces everything from a Polaris battery to an SRB sleeve. A genuine V072 part with no paperwork is still a genuine V072 part, and at the REAL clearing price ($50-275, per the table) it is a perfectly good display piece and an honest piece of the program. Just know which of the five questions you are paying for, because the seller is answering question one and charging you for question four.

Hope for the best, plan for the worst, and read the dataplate. The room in Muncie did, and the Polaris battery is probably still for sale.
