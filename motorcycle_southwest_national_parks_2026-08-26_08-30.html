Southwest National Parks Motorcycle Trip, Days 14 to 18. August 26 – 30, 2026.



Trip Day Fourteen – Wednesday, August 26, 2026: Mesa Verde National Park (77 miles) – Cliff Palace and motorcycle tour of the park

My wife and I went on a tour of the Cliff Palace, reservations were made on the 20th and there were spots still open, but don't expect to get in on the day you arrive, all the spots may be taken.

The Cliff Palace is the largest cliff dwelling in North America, about 150 rooms and 23 kivas built into a south-facing sandstone alcove home to about 100 Ancestral Puebloans. Their descendants include today’s Hopi, Zuni, Acoma and Rio Grande Pueblo people, and 26 tribes are culturally affiliated with Mesa Verde. Ranchers Richard Wetherill and Charlie Mason named it a palace when they came across it in December 1888 while looking for stray cattle. The Ancestral Puebloans had lived and farmed on the mesa tops since about AD 550 and moved down into the alcoves only in the late 1100s. The construction was between about 1190 and 1260, and the site was empty by 1300. The alcove was easier to defend and gave shelter. The low winter sun warmed the walls and the overhang shaded them in summer. It also left the mesa top free for fields of corn, beans and squash. The price was the daily climb: up to the fields on hand-and-toe holds cut into the cliff face, and down to the springs and seeps below the rim for water:


You walk on a path down to the Cliff Palace, yes you will be going inside where people walked about 800 years ago:


This is a kiva which is a round mostly underground room. You can see the ventilator shaft, the deflector stone that kept its draft off the fire, the firepit, and the pilasters that held up a roof of logs and earth. People entered by a ladder through a hatch in the roof, in many kivas a small hole was made north of the firepit, the sipapu, which represented the place where the people emerged into this world from the one below. Climbing down into a kiva and back up the ladder repeats that emergence. At Cliff Palace there is about one kiva for every handful of rooms. Archaeologists think most served a household or extended family group. They were used for weaving, toolmaking and keeping warm in winter as well as for ceremonies. Among present day Pueblo people, the descendants of the Mesa Verde builders, kivas are mainly sacred spaces for ceremonies, religious societies and community decisions. Pueblo society is traditionally matrilineal: you belong to your mother’s clan, and the house and fields pass through the women. When a man marries he moves into his wife’s household, but his religious duties stay with his own clan, his mother’s and sisters’ people, and he goes back to them for ceremonies. Trip Day Fifteen – Thursday, August 27, 2026: Mesa Verde National Park, day two (bike stayed parked) – Long House Tour

You climb into Long House using the ladders on the left of this picture – yes, you will be going inside where people walked about 800 years ago. Long House is the second-largest cliff dwelling in the park, with about 150 rooms and 21 kivas, and around 100 to 150 people lived here in the mid-1200s. The open plaza in front was likely a gathering place for the whole community, much like the ceremonial plazas in Pueblo villages today. At the back of the alcove a seep spring still keeps plants green and holds standing water – a steady water supply in the cool shade, right at their back door. Getting to the Long House is part of the journey. Long House is on Wetherill Mesa, reachable only by ranger tour (again, make your reservations early), about a 2.25-mile round trip with two 15-foot ladders inside the site:

Trip Day Sixteen – Friday, August 28, 2026: Mancos to Montrose, Colorado on the Million Dollar Highway (140 miles)

I have heard so much about the Million Dollar Highway, its history, and how it is not safe. I disagree. Yes, there are sheer drop-offs, hairpins posted at 15 mph, almost no shoulder, and long stretches with no guardrail (the usual explanation is that the snowplows need somewhere to push the snow – over the edge). This IS a road that demands your attention, but not a dangerous one. Ride it at the posted speeds, stay in your lane, and it is just a spectacular mountain road. The most dangerous thing was the other people on the road (didn't think I had left THAT much room for a safe pass ...). Loved riding it, LOTS of motorcycles around, fall in line and zipper formation (i.e. staggered), you know the drill. Lots more traffic, slow cars, construction. All in all an enjoyable ride.

A little lore: the road started in the 1880s as Otto Mears' toll road between Ouray and the silver mines at Red Mountain, carved into the cliffs above the Uncompahgre Gorge. It crosses three passes over 10,000 feet: Coal Bank, Molas, and Red Mountain at 11,018 ft. Nobody agrees on where the name came from. One story says it cost a million dollars a mile to build in the 1920s. Another says the fill dirt was mine tailings with a million dollars of gold ore still in it. My favorite is the early traveler who swore he wouldn't drive it again for a million dollars. The one the locals don't tell the tourists is that the missing guardrails are buried in the snowbanks, along with a cross for every flatlander who thought the 25 mph sign was just a suggestion.

Trip Day Eighteen – Sunday, August 30, 2026: Black Canyon of the Gunnison, South Rim (44 miles)

Rode the south rim of Black Canyon of the Gunnison, I looked into riding along the north rim but when you turn west on Black Canyon Road about 3 miles south of town the last 7 miles of that access road are gravel, and so is all of North Rim Drive. Only the first few miles off the highway are paved. The Park Service calls the gravel "typically in good condition and passable for most vehicle", so YMMV.
